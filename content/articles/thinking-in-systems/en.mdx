## I love watching how people talk to AI

Here's a small hobby of mine. I watch how people communicate with AI.

Not what AI answers. What *they ask*.

One person types three vague words, gets a vague answer, and decides "AI is useless." Another person writes a clear paragraph: what they want, why they want it, what the result should look like. They get something sharp. Then they push back, adjust, ask again. They're not delegating their brain. They're using it.

We're in a strange moment. AI went from "doesn't exist" to "mandatory in personal life and at work" in what feels like a blink. And in this moment, the way someone prompts tells me a lot about them. How clear they are. How vague or straightforward their language is. Whether they actually know what they want. Whether they plan to learn something or just hand the whole thing off.

It's all there, in one text box.

And the more I watch, the more I believe one thing: **critical thinking is becoming the most important skill we have.** And I don't mean the surface version. I mean the deep one. Clarity. Consequences. Choosing words carefully. Judging output instead of swallowing it.

That is exactly why I want to talk about this book.

## What the book is about

*Thinking in Systems: A Primer* was written by Donella Meadows, an environmental scientist and systems thinker. She passed away in 2001, and the book was put together from her drafts and published in 2008. That alone tells you something. This is a lifetime of thinking, compressed.

The big idea is simple to say and very hard to unsee:

**Everything is a system.** A body. A company. A family. A forest. A relationship. A habit. Your calendar. And yes, AI.

A system, in her words (paraphrasing here), is a set of things connected in a way that produces its own pattern of behavior over time. It has three parts: **elements**, **connections** between them, and a **purpose**. And the surprising part is that the connections and the purpose often matter more than the elements themselves.

She walks you through a few core tools:

- **Stocks and flows.** Think of a bathtub. The water in it is the stock. The tap filling it and the drain emptying it are the flows. Your energy, your money, your trust with a client, your followers. All bathtubs.
- **Feedback loops.** Some loops keep things stable (a thermostat). Some loops make things grow or collapse faster and faster (compound interest, a panic, a viral post).
- **Delays.** Results rarely show up right after the action. That gap is where most people lose patience, overreact, or give up.
- **Leverage points.** Places in a system where a small push changes a lot. And the surprise: the obvious places (numbers, budgets) are usually the weakest. The strong ones are things like information flows, rules, goals, and the way we think about the system in the first place.
- **System traps.** Patterns that catch smart people again and again. Fixes that backfire. Short-term wins that quietly drain the long term.

It's not a long book. It's not academic in tone either, which is rare for this subject. She writes like someone who genuinely wants you to see what she sees.

## What it changed for me

I'll be honest: it changed the way I look at *everything*.

Before, I saw events. This happened. That happened. Good day, bad day, a client said yes, a client said no.

After, I started seeing **what's happening at the same time underneath**. The pattern behind the events. The loop that keeps repeating. The delay I didn't account for. The thing I'm feeding without noticing.

It's a bit like putting on glasses after years of squinting. You can't un-see it.

And it didn't stay in one area. It leaked into everything:

- **Personal level.** Decisions. Discipline. Everyday habits. Why some routines hold and others collapse in two weeks.
- **Career.** Why I choose certain paths, and what each choice quietly opens or closes.
- **Building a business.** Entrepreneurship is basically systems all the way down: clients, cash, reputation, energy, time.
- **Relationships.** Every conversation is a loop. What I say changes what comes back.
- **Big moves.** If you want to move somewhere new, or run something, or change something, your job is to understand the patterns, follow them, and curate them.

Because here's what I took from it: **you can't stop a pattern. But if you can see it, you can predict it, prepare for it, and gently steer its direction.**

That's a different relationship with life. Less "why is this happening to me." More "okay, what is this system doing, and where can I help it go somewhere better."

## How it affects my mind

Mostly, it slowed me down in a good way.

Before I decide something, I now ask a few different questions:

- What does this touch besides the thing I'm looking at?
- What will this look like in three months, not three days?
- Am I fixing the problem or just moving it somewhere else?
- What am I feeding with this choice?

Decision-making is the part that matters most to me, especially if you're the kind of person who takes responsibility. When you own the outcome, you can't afford to think in straight lines.

And I'll say something that may be a little controversial: **I don't think critical thinking and systems thinking are separate things.** When you understand how events depend on each other, when you can hold the whole picture, you naturally make better decisions. You also see how your daily choices land on you, your environment, your business, your people, your family.

## The garden and the forest

Meadows talks about bigger systems, ecosystems, and this image stayed with me the most.

Picture a forest. Or a garden. Every move you make in it, here, there, over there, touches the whole thing. Water. Soil. Light. Insects. Roots you can't see.

You can clean that space. Build conditions where everything performs better, where it grows wider than you planned.

Or you can destroy it. And it often takes only a few decisions.

It works exactly the same way with yourself.

You can destroy yourself with a few small, repeated choices. Or you can build an environment that allows you to grow. Or you can leave things open and let randomness walk in, with a small number of possibilities and a different outcome behind each one, depending on what you chose.

## Ideas I keep coming back to

**1. The purpose of a system shows in what it does, not what it says.**\
Companies say one thing and behave another. People too. Watch the behavior.

**2. We love to look at the parts, but the connections are where the magic (and the trouble) lives.**\
You can swap every player in a team and, if the structure stays the same, you get the same result.

**3. Delays fool us.**\
We act, nothing seems to happen, we push harder, and then everything hits at once.

**4. Where you intervene matters more than how hard you push.**\
The strongest leverage points are rules, information, goals, and mindset. Not just "work more."

**5. Don't just predict. Dance.**\
Her closing idea, the one I love: you can't control a system, but you can learn its rhythm and move with it. Stay humble, keep learning, pay attention to what's real, and widen the circle of what you care about.

## And yes, AI is a system too

This is where it connects back to where I started.

AI has processes. Inputs. Loops. It responds to how you shape the conversation, and it shapes how you think in return. Every prompt is a small intervention. Every output you accept or reject is feedback.

If you understand systems, you stop treating AI like a vending machine. You start asking: *What is this system doing? What am I feeding it? Where is my leverage?*

That unlocks a new level of curating three things at once: **yourself, the people around you, and the AI.**

People who think in systems will use AI as a partner. People who don't will either over-trust it or throw it away.

## Who should read this book

Honestly? Anyone who's a human.

If you want the more specific answer:

- If you run a business, read it. You'll see why some fixes keep failing.
- If you lead people, read it. You'll stop blaming individuals for structural problems.
- If you're building anything, a product, a brand, a career, read it.
- If you work with AI every day, read it. It'll change how you ask, how you judge, and how you decide.
- If you feel stuck in the same pattern again and again, read it twice.

You don't need a science background. You need curiosity and a little patience.

---

## My recommendation

If you want to improve, and if you want to see the bigger picture, I really recommend this book.

It won't give you a quick hack. It will give you something slower and much more valuable: a new way of seeing. And once you've seen it, every decision you make gets a little more honest, a little more aware, and a lot more yours.

*Thinking in Systems: A Primer, Donella H. Meadows.*
